Week 1 · Ch. 1

What Is Leadership?

Week 1 · Ch. 1

Leadership vs. Management

Management
Focuses on processes, systems, and efficiency — keeping things running correctly
Driven by plans, budgets, and organizational structure
Answers the question: Are we doing things right?
Leadership
Focuses on people, vision, and change — moving others toward something better
Driven by values, influence, and relationships — not authority
Answers the question: Are we doing the right things?
Kouzes and Posner define leadership as a process of influence — enabling a group to work toward a shared goal
Discussion Questions
Do you have to be in a leadership position in order to be a leader within your organization? Why or why not?
Why are the mission and vision of your organization important to the leadership process?

Week 1 · Ch. 2

The Five Practices Framework

Kouzes and Posner studied thousands of leaders across industries and cultures over 30 years — the Five Practices are what consistently separated effective leaders from their peers
PracticeCore Idea
1. Model the WaySet the example by aligning your actions with shared values — show it, don't just say it
2. Inspire a Shared VisionPaint a compelling picture of the future and unite people around it
3. Challenge the ProcessInnovate, experiment, and take smart risks — progress requires stepping outside the familiar
4. Enable Others to ActFoster collaboration, build trust, and empower others to contribute their best work
5. Encourage the HeartRecognize contributions, celebrate successes, and create a sense of belonging
Discussion Questions
Of the five practices, which one are you most drawn to? Which makes the most sense to you and which are you most skeptical about?
Surveying your reading and personal experience, how would you define leadership?
Week 1 · Itay Talgam

Lead Like the Great Conductors

What Talgam Shows
Six conductors — six different leadership styles — all leading the same kind of organization toward the same goal
One leads with strict control and precise demands. Another leads with joy and humor, drawing out creativity. Another is nearly invisible — the music emerges from the group organically
There is no single "correct" way to lead — effective leadership is about choosing the right approach for the context, the people, and the goal
The Lesson
A conductor achieves harmony without saying a word — through presence, signals, and trust built over time
The most effective conductors created space for musicians to bring something — they didn't just execute orders
Leadership is communication: verbal, nonverbal, structural, and cultural
Discussion Question
After watching Talgam's video, what role should fun or control play in organizational leadership? Are there situations where each approach is more appropriate?

Clarify Values

Week 2 · Ch. 3

Why Values Come First

Personal Values
Values are the principles that guide decisions — especially under pressure, when no one is watching
Leaders who cannot clearly articulate their values cannot lead consistently — people don't know what to expect
Ask yourself: What lines will I not cross, even if it costs me? That answer is your value system
Organizational Values
Every organization has stated values (in mission statements) and practiced values (in daily behavior) — they are often different
The strongest leaders find where their personal values align with their organization's mission and lead from that overlap
Alignment creates authenticity. Misalignment creates friction — and eventually, either the leader leaves or the values erode
Discussion Questions
What do Kouzes and Posner mean by "Model the Way"? Can you think of an example from your own experience?

Week 2 · Ch. 4

Set the Example

Week 2 · Ch. 4

Credibility Is Built in Small Moments

How Leaders Set the Example
People watch what you do far more closely than they listen to what you say — inconsistency destroys credibility fast
Small actions signal values: how you respond to a mistake, how you treat someone with no power over you, whether you follow through on small commitments
Kouzes and Posner: "You cannot ask others to do what you are unwilling to do yourself"
Seth Godin: Tribes
A tribe is a group connected by a leader, a shared idea, and a common way of communicating
Anyone can start a movement by clearly living out their values — formal authority is not required
Leaders model the way for their tribes — consistent behavior is what creates followership, not title
Discussion Questions
What are some practical ways leaders set expectations for others?
What happens when personal values and organizational values are in conflict? How should leaders respond?

Envision the Future

Week 3 · Ch. 5

What Vision Actually Is

Vision Is Not a Goal
A vision is a story of possibility — where we are going, why it matters, and what role each person plays in getting there
Goals are milestones. Vision is the direction that gives those milestones meaning
Kouzes and Posner: effective leaders are forward-looking — this is consistently the quality followers rank as most important in leaders they admire
Looking Forward Requires Looking Back
Scanning past trends — technological, demographic, social, economic — gives leaders the grounding to chart a realistic bold course
Drucker's point: vision without organizational context is fantasy. A dream must be rooted in the reality of what the organization can actually do
What future trends will most affect your project? Demographics, technology, shifts in your organization's priorities?
Discussion Questions
What future trends — demographics, technology, regulations, or cultural changes — are likely to influence the direction of your project?

Week 3 · Ch. 6

Enlist Others

Week 3 · Ch. 6

Making the Vision Shared

Simon Sinek: Start with Why
The Golden Circle: Why → How → What
Most leaders communicate from the outside in — What we do, How we do it. Inspiring leaders start from the inside out — Why we exist
People don't follow because of what you do. They follow because of why you do it
Applied to your project: lead with purpose before explaining the plan
Derek Sivers: The First Follower
A movement begins not with the leader — it begins when the first person decides to join
The first follower transforms a lone actor into a leader. Nurturing that person is as important as the vision itself
Who is your first follower? Who needs to buy in before anyone else will?
Enlisting others means appealing to their values and interests — not just explaining your plan
Discussion Questions
Describe Simon Sinek's Golden Circle. How does it apply to your leadership approach?
Who might be your "first follower," and how can you encourage their commitment?

Week 4 · Ch. 7

Search for Opportunities

Week 4 · Ch. 7

Finding Where to Innovate

How Leaders Discover Opportunities
Opportunities often come from staying alert to small signals: recurring complaints, workflow inefficiencies, unmet customer needs, or new competitors entering the market
Limiting assumptions are the beliefs a team holds as fixed that quietly prevent them from seeing possibilities — "we've always done it this way" is the most dangerous phrase in any organization
Effective leaders ask "What if?" — they create space to surface assumptions and test them
Finding a Co-Conspirator
Dasgupta's core insight: change rarely happens in isolation — leaders need partners who share their values and are willing to challenge norms alongside them
A co-conspirator spreads ownership of the change, makes experimentation feel less risky, and builds credibility through shared commitment
Who in your project could serve as a co-conspirator? What would it take to bring them in?
Discussion Questions
What are some methods of discovering places for innovation in your organization or project?
Why should leaders check for "limiting assumptions"? What are they and where do they come from?

Week 4 · Ch. 8

Experiment and Take Risks

Week 4 · Ch. 8

Small Wins and Sustainable Change

The Power of Small Wins
A small win is a manageable change that demonstrates progress without overwhelming people or requiring massive risk
Each small win provides evidence that change is possible — it builds momentum and gives the team something to point to
Example: pilot a new process with one team before rolling it out organization-wide — learn from it before scaling
Learning from Mistakes
Experimentation means some things will not work — leaders who punish failure kill innovation before it starts
Frame setbacks as data: what did we learn, what do we adjust, what do we try next
Sustaining change requires: celebrating progress, gathering feedback, staying flexible, and consistently reconnecting the team to the shared values behind the effort
Discussion Questions
What does it mean to "generate small wins"? Share an example from your own experience.
Kouzes and Posner argue all change requires leadership. What can be done if senior leaders are not engaged in driving change?

Week 5 · Ch. 9

Foster Collaboration

Week 5 · Ch. 9

Building a Collaborative Environment

What Collaboration Requires
Collaboration doesn't happen by default — leaders must actively create conditions where people feel safe to share ideas and take risks together
Break down silos — "us vs. them" thinking between departments, generations, or roles is the enemy of coordinated effort
Ask questions that invite participation: "What do you think we should try next?" "What barriers are you facing?" These signals matter more than formal structures
Jim Tamm: Overcoming Defensiveness
Defensiveness is one of the biggest threats to collaboration — when people feel attacked or judged, they protect themselves instead of contributing
Leaders model openness — they respond to challenge with curiosity rather than self-protection
Colin Powell: "Leadership is solving problems. The day soldiers stop bringing you their problems is the day you have stopped leading them."
Discussion Questions

Week 5 · Ch. 10

Strengthen Others

Week 5 · Ch. 10

Empowering People to Lead Themselves

How Leaders Strengthen Others
The best leaders distribute power — they give people stretch assignments, real responsibility, and genuine decision-making authority
Providing opportunities: Let people practice leadership before they have a leadership title
Offering feedback: Specific, honest, and timely — both positive reinforcement and constructive critique help people grow
Mentoring: Share lessons from your own experience, then step back and let them find their own solutions
Reverse Mentorship
Patrice Gordon's concept: pair junior employees with senior leaders so learning flows in both directions
Junior employees bring fresh perspectives, digital fluency, and unfiltered insight about culture — things senior leaders often lose access to
Reverse mentorship builds confidence in younger team members and keeps senior leaders connected to ground-level reality
Discussion Questions
Kouzes and Posner list multiple ways leaders can strengthen others. Which strategy resonates most with you, and why?
Could a reverse mentorship program work in your organization? What benefits or challenges would it bring?

Week 6 · Ch. 11 & 12

Encourage the Heart

Week 6 · Ch. 11 & 12

Recognition That Actually Works

Why Recognition Matters
Kouzes and Posner: leaders must both expect the best from people and recognize them when they deliver — expectation alone is not enough
Recognition is not about being nice — it is about sustaining the motivation and commitment that drives results over time
How you acknowledge contributions shapes the team's identity and reinforces shared values more than any policy document
How to Recognize Well
Be specific: "Your attention to detail on that forecast prevented us from missing a critical issue" lands harder than "good job"
Make it personal: Some people thrive on public recognition; others prefer a direct word or note — know your team
Connect to values: Tie recognition to what the team stands for — "This is exactly what we mean when we say we put clients first"
Discussion Questions
What does "encouraging the heart" mean to you personally? How does it differ from simply "being nice"?
Think of a time when someone recognized your contributions. What made that recognition meaningful — or not?
Week 6 · Ch. 12 & 13

Celebrating Values and Victories

Celebrations Reinforce Culture
Celebrations don't require expense — they require intentionality. Sharing a team win at the start of a meeting, a personal thank-you note, or a small team ritual all count
What you celebrate signals what you value. Teams observe this closely
Celebrations should be genuine, frequent, and values-based — not performative or forced
McChrystal: Leadership Is a Choice
Leadership is not a position you are given — it is a posture you choose to take in each moment
Anyone can choose to lead — to take responsibility, to model values, to serve others — regardless of formal authority
This reframes the entire course: the Five Practices are not a checklist for people with titles. They are a set of daily choices available to everyone
Discussion Questions
What are some creative, low-cost ways to celebrate team victories in a professional setting?
General McChrystal talks about leadership as a choice. How does this perspective change your understanding of leadership?
