Economic Systems
and Business

Week 1 · Ch. 1

How Economies Work

Economics studies how societies allocate scarce resources — every business decision is made within an economic system
Free market: Prices set by supply and demand with minimal government interference — the U.S. model
Command economy: Government controls production and pricing — limited competition or innovation
Mixed economy: Most real economies combine market forces with some government regulation
Microeconomics: Behavior of individual firms, consumers, and markets — prices, production, competition
Macroeconomics: The broader economy — GDP, inflation, unemployment, national output
Week 1 · Ch. 1

Measuring Economic Health

Key Economic Indicators
GDP (Gross Domestic Product): Total value of all goods and services produced — primary measure of economic size and growth
Employment rate: High employment signals a healthy economy; unemployment above ~5% signals problems
Inflation: Rising prices over time — mild inflation (2-3%) is normal; high inflation erodes purchasing power
Price stability: The Federal Reserve targets ~2% annual inflation as ideal
Government Policy Tools
Monetary policy (Federal Reserve): Controls money supply and interest rates — raises rates to slow inflation, lowers them to stimulate growth
Fiscal policy (Congress + President): Controls government spending and taxation — spending increases stimulate; tax increases slow the economy
Business cycles include expansion, peak, contraction, and trough — companies must plan for all phases
Discussion Questions
How do economic growth, full employment, price stability, and inflation indicate a nation's economic health?
How does the government use monetary policy and fiscal policy to achieve its macroeconomic goals?
Which trends are reshaping the business, microeconomic, and macroeconomic environments and competitive arena?

Business Ethics and
Social Responsibility

Week 1 · Ch. 2

What Ethical Business Behavior Looks Like

Ethics in business = doing what is right, legal, and fair — even when no one is watching
Sources of ethical guidance: Personal values, organizational codes of conduct, industry standards, and law
Code of ethics: A formal document outlining standards of behavior expected of all employees
Whistleblowing: Reporting unethical behavior internally or externally — legally protected in many contexts
Ethical failures create legal liability, financial loss, and lasting reputational damage — Enron, Wells Fargo, Theranos as examples
Organizations encourage ethics through training, clear reporting channels, leadership modeling, and consistent enforcement
Week 1 · Ch. 2

Social Responsibility and Stakeholders

Social Responsibility
Businesses have obligations beyond profit — to employees, customers, communities, and the environment
Carroll's Pyramid: Economic → Legal → Ethical → Philanthropic responsibilities (from base to peak)
ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance): Framework investors use to evaluate company responsibility and long-term risk
Sustainability reporting is now standard for public companies — stakeholders demand transparency
Who Are the Stakeholders?
Employees: Safe working conditions, fair pay, advancement opportunities
Customers: Safe products, honest advertising, fair pricing
Investors: Accurate financial reporting, sound management
Community: Jobs, taxes, environmental stewardship
Government: Legal compliance, tax obligations
Discussion Questions
How can organizations encourage ethical business behavior?
How do businesses meet their social responsibilities to various stakeholders? What are the trends in ethics and social responsibility?

Competing in the
Global Marketplace

Week 1 · Ch. 3

Why Nations Trade and the Barriers They Face

Why Nations Trade
Comparative advantage: Countries produce what they make most efficiently and trade for the rest
Access to resources, technology, and labor not available domestically
Larger potential market — more customers, more revenue, more growth
Trade agreements (NAFTA/USMCA, WTO) reduce barriers and expand access
Barriers to Trade
Tariffs: Taxes on imports — raise prices and protect domestic industries
Quotas: Limits on the quantity of imports allowed
Currency risk: Exchange rate fluctuations affect the cost of imports and value of exports
Cultural differences: Language, norms, business practices, and consumer preferences vary widely
Political and legal risks: Regulations, corruption, expropriation, and instability
Week 1 · Ch. 3

How Companies Enter Global Markets

Exporting: Selling goods produced at home to buyers in other countries — lowest commitment, lowest risk, lowest control
Licensing: Granting a foreign company the right to use your brand, patent, or process for a royalty fee
Franchising: Licensing a complete business system — McDonald's, Subway, and most hotel chains use this model globally
Joint venture: Partnering with a local company — shared risk, shared knowledge, shared control
Direct investment / foreign subsidiary: Building or buying operations in another country — highest commitment, highest control, highest risk
Choice of entry strategy depends on: risk tolerance, capital available, need for local knowledge, and importance of control
Discussion Questions
Why do nations trade? What are the barriers to international trade?
How do companies enter the global marketplace? What are the trends in the global marketplace?

Forms of
Business Ownership

Week 2 · Ch. 4

Sole Proprietorships and Partnerships

Sole Proprietorship
One owner, complete control, and all profits — the simplest and most common form of business
Easy to form — no special legal requirements in most states
Unlimited personal liability: Owner is personally responsible for all debts and legal judgments — your house, savings, and assets are at risk
Difficult to raise capital — limited to owner's savings and personal loans
Business ends when owner retires, dies, or sells
Partnership
Two or more owners share control, profits, and responsibilities
General partnership: All partners have equal liability for business debts
Limited partnership (LP): Silent partners invest but have limited liability — general partner runs the business
LLP (Limited Liability Partnership): All partners have limited liability — common for law firms and accountants
Partnership agreement is essential — defines roles, profit sharing, and exit procedures
Discussion Questions
What are the advantages and disadvantages of the sole proprietorship form of business?
What are the advantages of operating as a partnership, and what downside risks should partners consider?
Week 2 · Ch. 4

Corporations, LLCs, and Franchising

Corporations
Limited liability: Shareholders can lose their investment, but not their personal assets
Can raise capital by issuing stock — publicly traded or privately held
Complex structure: Board of Directors → CEO → Officers → Employees
C-Corp: Double taxation — taxed at corporate level and again when dividends are paid to shareholders
S-Corp: Pass-through taxation — profits flow directly to shareholders' personal returns; limited to 100 shareholders
LLC: Combines limited liability of a corporation with the tax flexibility of a partnership — increasingly popular for small business
Franchising
License a proven brand, system, and support in exchange for fees and royalties
Advantages: Built-in brand recognition, proven business model, training and support from franchisor
Disadvantages: Less control over operations, required to follow franchisor rules, ongoing royalty payments
Examples: McDonald's, Subway, 7-Eleven, UPS Store, Marriott hotels
Franchising continues to grow — lower failure rate than independent startups
Discussion Questions
How does the corporate structure provide advantages and disadvantages? What are the major types of corporations?
What makes franchising an appropriate form for some business types, and why does it continue to grow? What trends will affect future business organizations?

Starting Your Own Business

Week 2 · Ch. 5

What Entrepreneurs Do and Why

The Entrepreneurial Mindset
Entrepreneurs identify opportunities where others see problems or gaps in the market
They take calculated risks — not reckless ones. The goal is managing uncertainty, not eliminating it
Key traits: Initiative, resilience, creativity, adaptability, comfort with ambiguity
Small businesses employ nearly half of the private-sector workforce in the U.S.
Entrepreneurs drive innovation — they are not constrained by existing corporate structures and incentives
Starting and Growing a Business
Business plan: The essential roadmap — describes the business, market, competition, operations, and financials
Business plan is also required to secure funding from banks and investors
Common startup challenges: Access to capital, competition from established players, attracting talent, and scaling efficiently
Most small businesses fail within 5 years — poor planning, undercapitalization, and weak market research are top causes
Resources: SBA loans, SCORE mentorship, angel investors, venture capital for high-growth startups
Discussion Questions
Why do people become entrepreneurs? Which characteristics do successful entrepreneurs share?
What are the advantages and disadvantages facing owners of small businesses? What trends shape entrepreneurship?

The Four Functions of Management

Planning and Organizing
Planning: Setting goals, determining how to achieve them, and preparing for the future — strategic, tactical, and operational plans
Organizing: Arranging resources and tasks — who does what, who reports to whom, how decisions are made
Planning without organizing leads to great ideas and no execution; organizing without planning leads to efficient work on the wrong things
Leading and Controlling
Leading: Motivating and guiding people toward goals — requires communication, inspiration, and emotional intelligence
Controlling: Monitoring performance, comparing to goals, and making corrections — the feedback loop of management
Types of power: Legitimate (title), Reward (incentives), Coercive (punishment), Expert (knowledge), Referent (respect and admiration)
Effective managers use all five types — overreliance on coercive power destroys morale and trust
Discussion Questions
How would you define management? What roles do managers take on in different organizational settings?

Week 3 · Ch. 6

Leadership Styles

Autocratic leadership: Leader makes decisions unilaterally — fast in a crisis, but kills employee initiative and engagement over time
Democratic (participative) leadership: Employees are involved in decision-making — higher engagement, slower decisions
Laissez-faire leadership: Leader steps back and lets employees direct themselves — works with highly skilled, self-motivated teams; dangerous otherwise
Transformational leadership: Inspires employees to exceed expectations by appealing to higher purpose and vision
Transactional leadership: Motivates through rewards and punishments tied to performance — clear expectations, clear consequences
The best leaders adapt their style to the situation — no single style works in every context
Week 3 · Ch. 7

Designing Organizational Structures

Key Structural Concepts
Hierarchy: Levels of authority from top to bottom — determines who has power and who makes decisions
Span of control: How many employees one manager oversees — wider spans save cost; narrower spans allow closer supervision
Centralization: Decisions made at the top — consistency and control; slower response to local needs
Decentralization: Decisions pushed down the organization — faster, more responsive to local conditions
Common Structures
Functional: Organized by department (Marketing, Finance, Operations) — efficiency within functions, but silos between them
Divisional: Organized by product, geography, or customer type — each division is semi-autonomous
Matrix: Employees report to both a functional manager and a project manager — maximizes flexibility, creates confusion
Flat organizations (fewer layers) increase speed and accountability; tall organizations provide more supervision and control
Discussion Questions
Based on your reading, how would you describe your organization's structure?
What trends are influencing the way businesses organize? (Remote work, agile teams, gig economy)
Week 3 · Ch. 8

Managing Human Resources and Labor Relations

The HR Function
HR manages the full employee lifecycle: recruit → select → onboard → train → evaluate → compensate → retain or separate
Job analysis: Defining the tasks, skills, and qualifications for every role — the foundation of hiring and evaluation
Selection tools: Interviews, skills tests, background checks, reference checks — each must be legally defensible
Performance appraisals: 360-degree reviews, MBO (Management by Objectives), or rating scales
Labor Relations and Trends
Unions: Represent workers in collective bargaining — negotiate wages, hours, and working conditions
Collective bargaining: Process of negotiating a contract between union and management
Diversity, equity, and inclusion are now strategic priorities — not just compliance requirements
Trends: remote hiring, AI in recruiting, gig economy worker classification, pay transparency laws
Discussion Question

Week 3 · Ch. 9

Motivation Theories

Need-Based Theories
Maslow's Hierarchy: Physiological → Safety → Social/Belonging → Esteem → Self-Actualization — lower needs must be met before higher ones motivate
Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory: Hygiene factors (pay, working conditions, job security) prevent dissatisfaction but don't motivate. True motivators: achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth
McClelland's Acquired Needs: People are motivated by achievement, affiliation, or power — managers need to match incentives to employee drives
Process-Based Theories
Expectancy Theory (Vroom): Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence — employees must believe effort leads to performance, performance leads to reward, and the reward is worth having
Goal-Setting Theory: Specific, challenging, accepted goals drive higher performance than vague "do your best" goals
Practical tools: recognition programs, autonomy, development opportunities, flexible work, fair and transparent compensation
Discussion Questions

Week 3 · Ch. 10

Operations Management

What Operations Managers Do
Operations management oversees the production of goods and services — efficiently, at quality, and at cost
Supply chain management: Coordinates suppliers, production, and delivery — a disrupted supply chain can shut down a company
Inventory management: Too much inventory = wasted capital; too little = missed sales and unhappy customers
Capacity planning: Ensuring production capacity matches current and future demand
Quality Frameworks
TQM (Total Quality Management): Organization-wide commitment to continuous improvement at every stage — quality is everyone's job
Six Sigma: Data-driven approach to eliminating defects — targets fewer than 3.4 defects per million opportunities
Lean manufacturing: Eliminate waste in every form — excess inventory, unnecessary motion, waiting time, overproduction
ISO certification: International standards that signal consistent quality processes to global customers
Discussion Questions
Why are resource-planning tasks like inventory management and supplier relations critical to production?
Based on your reading, how would you define operations management?

Marketing Strategy and Consumer Behavior

Building a Marketing Strategy
Marketing strategy starts with understanding who your customer is, what they need, and how your product solves their problem
Market segmentation: Divide the market into groups with shared characteristics — demographic, geographic, psychographic, behavioral
Target market: The specific segment(s) the company chooses to serve — you can't serve everyone well
Positioning: How you want customers to perceive your brand relative to competitors — this is communicated through the entire marketing mix
Consumer Buying Decisions
B2C buying process: Problem recognition → Information search → Evaluation → Purchase → Post-purchase evaluation
High involvement: Complex purchases (cars, homes) require extensive research and comparison
Low involvement: Habitual purchases (toothpaste, coffee) rely on brand loyalty and convenience
Marketers design messages for specific stages — awareness campaigns target early stages; promotions close sales at the end
Discussion Questions
How do managers create a marketing strategy? How do consumers and organizations make buying decisions?
Week 4 · Ch. 11

Products, Pricing, and Branding

Product Development and Lifecycle
New product development: Idea generation → Screening → Concept testing → Strategy development → Business analysis → Development → Test marketing → Launch
Introduction: Low sales, high costs — build awareness
Growth: Sales climb, competitors enter — differentiate
Maturity: Sales peak — defend market share and extend the product
Decline: Sales drop — harvest profits or exit the market
Pricing and Branding
Cost-plus pricing: Add a profit margin to your costs — simple but ignores what customers will pay
Value-based pricing: Price reflects customer's perceived value — can command premium prices
Penetration pricing: Low initial price to build market share quickly
Price skimming: High initial price for new or innovative products, then lower as competition enters
Brand equity: The premium customers pay because of brand reputation — Apple, Nike, and Rolex command prices competitors can't match
Discussion Questions
How do organizations create new products? What strategies are used for pricing products?
Week 4 · Ch. 12

Distribution and Promotion

Distribution Channels
Distribution channels move products from producer to consumer — the path a product takes to reach its buyer
Direct channel: Producer sells directly to consumer — Dell computers, Tesla, most e-commerce
Indirect channel: Producer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer — most consumer packaged goods
Retail types: Department, specialty, discount, supermarket, warehouse club, e-commerce, convenience
Supply chain management coordinates suppliers, production, and delivery to maximize efficiency and customer satisfaction
The Promotion Mix
Advertising: Paid, non-personal communication — builds awareness and brand image at scale
Public relations: Earned media — press coverage, community involvement, crisis communication
Personal selling: One-on-one interaction — expensive but most effective for complex, high-value sales
Sales promotions: Short-term incentives — coupons, rebates, free samples, contests
IMC (Integrated Marketing Communications): All channels must deliver a consistent brand message
Discussion Questions
What are the different kinds of retail operations? How can supply chain management increase efficiency?
How would you define promotion? How does public relations fit into the promotional mix?

Information Technology in Business

Core IT Systems
ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning): Integrates all business processes — finance, HR, supply chain, manufacturing — into one unified system. SAP and Oracle are the dominant players.

Week 5 · Ch. 14

The Three Core Financial Statements

Balance Sheet
Snapshot of financial position at a specific point in time
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity — this equation must always balance
Assets: What the company owns — cash, inventory, equipment, property
Liabilities: What the company owes — loans, accounts payable, bonds
Equity: What belongs to the owners after all debts are paid
Income Statement and Cash Flow
Income statement: Revenue minus expenses equals net profit (or loss) — covers a period of time (quarter, year)
Gross profit = Revenue minus cost of goods sold. Operating profit subtracts operating expenses. Net income subtracts taxes and interest.
Cash flow statement: Tracks actual cash in and out — a company can show accounting profit but still run out of cash
Three sections: Operating activities, investing activities, financing activities
GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) and FASB ensure consistent reporting across companies
Discussion Questions
Who in an organization needs access to financial reporting? How does a balance sheet describe an organization?
How does the income statement report a firm's profitability?
Week 5 · Ch. 14

Using Ratio Analysis to Evaluate Financial Health

Liquidity Ratios
Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities — measures ability to pay short-term obligations. Above 2.0 is generally healthy.
Quick ratio (Acid test): Excludes inventory — more conservative measure of immediate liquidity
Profitability Ratios
Net profit margin = Net income ÷ Revenue — how much of each dollar of revenue becomes profit
Return on equity (ROE): Profit generated for each dollar of owner investment
Leverage Ratios
Debt-to-equity ratio = Total debt ÷ Total equity — how much the company is financed by debt vs. owner investment
High leverage amplifies gains in good times and losses in bad times — a key risk indicator
Activity Ratios
Inventory turnover: How many times inventory is sold and replaced in a period — high turnover signals strong sales and efficient inventory management
Ratios are most useful when compared to industry benchmarks and the company's own historical performance
Discussion Question
How can ratio analysis be used to identify a firm's financial strengths and weaknesses?

Money and Financial Institutions

What Money Does
Medium of exchange: Makes transactions possible — eliminates the inefficiency of barter
Store of value: Allows wealth to be saved and transferred over time
Unit of account: Provides a common measure to compare the value of different goods and services
Money supply: M1 (cash + checking), M2 (M1 + savings + money market) — the Fed controls this to manage the economy
Types of Financial Institutions
Commercial banks: Accept deposits, make loans, facilitate payments — serve individuals and businesses
Credit unions: Member-owned, non-profit cooperatives — typically offer better rates than commercial banks
Investment banks: Help companies raise capital, manage M&A transactions, underwrite stock and bond offerings
Federal Reserve: Central bank of the U.S. — controls monetary policy, supervises banks, maintains financial system stability
FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000 — protects consumers and prevents bank runs

Week 6 · Ch. 16

Financial Management and Securities Markets

What Financial Managers Do
Financial managers plan the company's capital needs, manage cash flow, and make investment decisions
Short-term decisions: Managing accounts receivable, accounts payable, and inventory to maintain sufficient liquidity
Long-term decisions (capital budgeting): Evaluating major investments in equipment, facilities, and technology using NPV and IRR
Sources of capital: Equity (selling stock), debt (issuing bonds or borrowing), retained earnings, venture capital
Securities Markets
NYSE and NASDAQ: Allow public companies to sell stock and raise capital from millions of investors
IPO (Initial Public Offering): First time a company sells shares to the public — raises large capital but requires ongoing disclosure
Stocks: Represent ownership; Bonds: Represent debt — different risk/return profiles
Finance affects every aspect of firm strategy — growth plans, hiring, product development, and pricing all depend on capital availability
Discussion Questions
How do finance and the financial manager affect the firm's overall strategy?

Developing a Career in Business

Building Career Capital
Career success requires self-awareness, networking, and continuous learning — not just technical skills
Business professionals benefit most from developing: communication, analytical thinking, leadership, and financial literacy
Internships, mentors, and professional associations accelerate career growth significantly — start building these relationships now
LinkedIn profile, resume, and interview skills are foundational tools — non-optional for any career path
The Changing Workplace
The business landscape evolves constantly — adaptability is the most durable competitive advantage any professional can build
AI and automation will replace routine tasks — the careers with staying power require judgment, creativity, and relationship skills
Continuous learning is not optional — professional certifications, online courses, and industry reading keep skills current
Your personal brand is built by consistent behavior, reputation, and the quality of your work — start building it deliberately
