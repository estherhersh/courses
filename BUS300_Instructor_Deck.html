Week 1 · Ch. 1

What Marketing Really Is

Marketing is the process of creating, communicating, and delivering value to customers — it is a strategic function that touches every part of an organization, not just advertising or sales
Product: What are you offering? How is it designed, packaged, and positioned in the market?
Price: What are customers willing to pay? How do you balance value delivered with costs incurred?
Place: Where and how is the product available? Which channels reach your target customer most efficiently?
Promotion: How do you communicate the value proposition to the right people at the right time?
Marketing operates within a broader environment — economic, social, technological, and legal forces all shape what's possible and what's effective
Customer satisfaction and long-term loyalty are built through consistent, genuine value delivery — not just clever campaigns

Week 1 · Ch. 3

The Consumer Decision Process

5 Stages of the Decision Process
1. Problem recognition: The consumer realizes a need or desire — either internally triggered (hunger) or externally triggered (an ad)
2. Information search: Internal search (memory and experience) followed by external search (online reviews, recommendations, ads)
3. Evaluation of alternatives: Comparing options on key attributes — price, quality, brand, convenience
4. Purchase decision: The actual buying act — can be influenced at the last moment by a promotion or a negative review
5. Post-purchase evaluation: Did the product meet expectations? Determines future loyalty and word-of-mouth
Types of Buying Behavior
Complex: High involvement, significant differences between brands (car, home, laptop) — extensive research
Dissonance-reducing: High involvement, few perceived differences (carpet, appliances) — buy then seek reassurance
Habitual: Low involvement, low brand differentiation (toothpaste, bread) — routine repurchase with minimal thought
Variety-seeking: Low involvement, high brand switching (snacks, shampoo) — change for novelty, not dissatisfaction
Week 1 · Ch. 3

What Influences Consumer Decisions

External Influences
Cultural factors: Values, beliefs, customs, and traditions that shape what people want and how they buy — the deepest and most pervasive influence
Subculture: Groups within a culture — ethnic background, religion, geographic region, age cohort (Boomers, Gen Z)
Social factors: Family, peer groups, reference groups, and social class — "what will people think?"
Opinion leaders and influencers have outsized impact on purchase decisions in their categories
Internal Influences
Personal factors: Age, life stage, income, occupation, lifestyle, personality — all shape preferences and purchasing power
Psychological factors: Motivation (Maslow's hierarchy), perception (selective attention), learning (brand associations), and attitudes
Situational factors: Time pressure, store atmosphere, occasion, mood — can override rational decision-making
Discussion Questions
Businesses send thousands of marketing messages each day. Which are most influential in your life right now? Provide an example of selective attention for each: social, personal, psychological, cultural, and situational.

B2B vs. B2C: Two Different Worlds

B2C Markets
Marketing directly to individual consumers for personal use — clothing, food, entertainment, personal services
Decisions often driven by emotion, habit, brand loyalty, or personal preference
Shorter buying cycle, smaller transaction values, mass marketing approaches common
Buyer and user are often the same person — or same household
B2B Markets
Organizations buying from organizations — tech companies buying software, hospitals buying equipment, schools buying supplies
Purchases are larger in scale, more rational, and more formally structured
Buying center: Multiple stakeholders with different roles — initiator, influencer, decider, buyer, gatekeeper, user
8-step formal process: Problem recognition → Need description → Product specification → Supplier search → Proposal solicitation → Supplier selection → Order routine → Performance review
Relationships and long-term value matter far more than in B2C — retention is the priority

Week 2 · Ch. 5

Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning

Segmentation
Demographic: Age, gender, income, education, family size — the most commonly used and most measurable
Geographic: Region, country, city size, climate — where people live shapes what they need
Psychographic: Values, lifestyle, personality, activities, interests — who they are beyond demographics
Behavioral: Usage rate, brand loyalty, purchase occasion, benefits sought — what they do with the product
Targeting and Positioning
Targeting: Select the segments with the best combination of size, growth potential, profitability, and fit with company strengths
Strategies: Undifferentiated (mass), Differentiated (multiple segments), Concentrated (one niche), Micromarketing (individual)
Positioning: The place your product occupies in the mind of your target customer relative to competitors
Perceptual map: Plots your brand against competitors on two key attributes — reveals opportunities and competitive crowding
Buyer persona: A fictional but realistic profile of your ideal customer — gives the market segment a face, a job, a life
Discussion Questions
Discuss methods of segmenting consumer markets. Why would a local hair salon choose geographic segmentation and a narrow concentrated strategy? Might multi-segment marketing be more effective?
What is a target market? How does it relate to product positioning?
Week 2 · Ch. 6

Marketing Research and Market Intelligence

The Research Process
Step 1: Define the problem and research objectives — the most critical step; wrong question = useless data
Step 2: Develop the research plan — choose methods, sources, and budget
Steps 3-4: Collect and analyze data
Steps 5-7: Interpret findings, present results, make decisions
Primary data: Collected directly — surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, experiments
Secondary data: Already exists — government reports, industry studies, internal sales records, competitor analysis
Intelligence and Ethics
Marketing intelligence: Everyday information about developments in the marketing environment — competitor monitoring, industry news, social listening
Big data: Massive datasets that reveal behavioral patterns at scale — powers personalization, dynamic pricing, and predictive analytics
Ethics in data collection: Consumer privacy is a legal and reputational issue — GDPR in Europe, CCPA in California signal growing regulation
Companies like Meta face ongoing scrutiny for data practices — the cost of misuse is enormous
Discussion Questions
As a consumer, how do you feel about companies tracing your digital footprint? As a marketer, what value do you see in tracing consumers' online activities?
Why might quantitative research be better for measuring attitudes toward new products? Why might qualitative data provide deeper insights into consumer behavior?

Week 3 · Ch. 7

Global Marketing Strategy

Market Entry Strategies
Exporting: Produce at home, sell abroad — lowest cost, lowest control, lowest risk
Licensing: Grant a foreign company rights to use your brand or process for royalties — low investment, low control
Franchising: License a complete business system — consistent global brand with local operators
Joint venture: Partner with a local company — shared risk, shared knowledge, shared profits
Direct investment: Build or buy operations in the foreign market — highest control, highest commitment, highest risk
Assessing Global Markets
Cultural environment: Language, religion, customs, attitudes toward foreign brands — underestimating culture is the #1 cause of global marketing failure
Legal environment: Data privacy laws (GDPR), trade regulations, IP protection vary dramatically by country
Economic environment: GDP per capita, income distribution, inflation, infrastructure — determines purchasing power
FCPA (Foreign Corrupt Practices Act): U.S. law prohibiting bribery of foreign officials — applies to all U.S. companies operating abroad
Standardization vs. adaptation: Global consistency vs. local relevance — most companies find a middle path

Week 3 · Ch. 8

Diversity Marketing in the U.S.

The Shifting U.S. Market
The U.S. is becoming more multicultural, multigenerational, and multidimensional — demographics are shifting rapidly
Hispanic Americans: fastest-growing consumer segment; $2+ trillion in annual purchasing power
Black consumers: $1.8 trillion in purchasing power; strong brand loyalty when brands show genuine commitment
Gen Z: First truly digital-native generation — brand values and social responsibility are purchasing criteria, not differentiators
LGBTQ+ visibility and representation: Significant buying power; highly brand loyal to companies that show authentic support
Authentic vs. Performative Marketing
Diversity marketing acknowledges and embraces the unique values, languages, and needs of different consumer groups
Authenticity is the dividing line: Brands that genuinely commit to communities build loyalty. Brands that adopt causes as trends get called out.
"Rainbow washing" (LGBTQ+), "greenwashing" (environment), and "woke washing" (social justice) all damage brand trust when detected
Examples of authentic: Nike (Kaepernick), Dove (Real Beauty), Ben & Jerry's (social advocacy)
When people see themselves genuinely reflected in a brand's message, trust and loyalty grow — this is both ethical and strategic
Discussion Questions
When should you use diversity marketing as an adaptation strategy? What are the benefits and challenges of a diverse marketplace?

Products, Lifecycle, and Brand Strategy

Product Classification
Convenience: Bought frequently with minimal effort — staples (milk, bread), impulse (candy bar), emergency (umbrella in rain)
Shopping: Purchased less frequently, consumers compare options — clothing, electronics, furniture
Specialty: Unique characteristics, brand identity so strong buyers will seek them out — Rolex, Tesla, luxury goods
Unsought: Consumers don't know they need them until a problem occurs — life insurance, fire extinguishers
Product Lifecycle Strategy
Introduction: Low sales, high costs — focus on building awareness; promotional spending is high
Growth: Sales accelerate, competitors enter — differentiate, expand distribution, refine messaging
Maturity: Sales peak, market saturates — compete on differentiation, promotions, and brand loyalty programs
Decline: Sales drop as newer solutions emerge — harvest cash, exit, or reinvention
Brand equity: The premium value a brand name adds — Apple charges more than identical hardware because of brand. Protect it obsessively.

Week 4 · Ch. 10

New Product Development

Stage 1 — Idea generation: Internal R&D, customer feedback, competitor analysis, crowdsourcing — the wider the funnel the better
Stage 2 — Screening and evaluation: Filter ideas against strategic fit, resource availability, and market potential — most ideas fail here, and that's good
Stage 3 — Concept testing: Present the idea (not the product) to target customers — do they understand it? Would they buy it?
Stage 4 — Marketing strategy development: Define target market, positioning, pricing, distribution, and promotion plans
Stage 5 — Business analysis: Estimate sales, costs, and profitability — does this make financial sense at scale?
Stage 6 — Product development: Build the actual product — engineering, design, and testing. First time real money is committed.
Stage 7 — Test marketing and commercialization: Limited launch in select markets, then full rollout — skipping this step is a common and costly mistake
Discussion Question
What should we know about new product development and marketing? What role does innovation play in maintaining a competitive edge?
Week 4 · Ch. 11

Marketing Services

Four Characteristics of Services
Intangibility: Cannot be seen, touched, or tested before purchase — customers buy a promise. Marketing must build trust.
Inseparability: Production and consumption happen simultaneously — the hotel room experience happens while you're staying in it
Perishability: Cannot be stored for later — an empty airplane seat at takeoff is revenue lost forever; hence dynamic pricing
Variability: Quality depends on who delivers it, when, and under what conditions — standardization through training is the solution
Managing Service Quality
Gap Model of Service Quality: Identifies 5 gaps between customer expectations and actual service delivery — each gap is a failure point
Example: Promising "24/7 customer support" but only staffing email during business hours = a trust gap that destroys loyalty
Internal marketing: Marketing to your own employees — engaged, trained employees create better customer experiences
Employee satisfaction drives customer satisfaction — Ritz-Carlton, Southwest Airlines, and Zappos built their brands on this principle
Discussion Questions
How do we classify services? What are some of the ethical considerations when providing services?
Week 4 · Ch. 12

Pricing Strategy

Pricing Strategies
Cost-based: Add a profit margin to cost — simple but ignores what customers are actually willing to pay
Value-based: Price reflects customer's perceived value — can sustain premium prices; requires deep customer understanding
Competition-based: Price relative to what competitors charge — industry benchmarking
Dynamic pricing: Prices change based on demand, time, or customer segment — airlines, Uber, Amazon all use this
Penetration pricing: Enter market with a low price to build share quickly, then raise it
Price skimming: High initial price for innovative product, lower as market matures — recoup R&D investment early
Psychological Pricing and Ethics
Odd-even pricing: $9.99 vs $10.00 — the left digit effect makes $9.99 feel significantly cheaper
Prestige pricing: High price signals luxury and quality — lowering the price of a Rolex would destroy the brand
Bundle pricing: Combine products at a discount — increases average transaction value
Price fixing: Agreement between competitors to set prices — illegal per se under antitrust law
Price gouging: Exploiting emergencies to charge excessive prices — illegal in many states, always a reputational disaster
Discussion Questions
What elements should we consider when pricing a product or service? Does the strategy for pricing change when something is a new product?
What is the promotion mix?

Week 5 · Ch. 13

Integrated Marketing Communications (IMC)

What IMC Is
IMC ensures all promotional tools, messaging, and media work together in harmony — every customer touchpoint tells the same story
It's more than visual consistency (logo, colors) — it's consistency of tone, promise, and value proposition across every channel
Without IMC: customers get conflicting messages — a premium brand running a discount ad undermines its positioning
The Communication Process
Sender: The brand or company
Encoding: Translating the idea into words, images, and sounds — creative execution
Message: The actual content delivered
Medium/Channel: TV, social, email, search, OOH, print
Decoding: How the receiver interprets the message — not always what was intended
Noise: Anything that distorts the message — competing messages, poor channel fit, unclear language, cultural differences
Feedback: Purchases, clicks, shares, reviews — how the audience responds tells you whether it worked

Week 5 · Ch. 14

Advertising and Public Relations

Advertising
Paid, non-personal communication from an identified source — the company controls the message completely
Ad planning: Set objectives → Determine budget → Choose media → Develop creative → Execute → Measure
Key metrics: Impressions (reach), Click-through rate (CTR), Conversion rate, Cost per acquisition (CPA), Return on ad spend (ROAS)
Digital advertising allows precise targeting and real-time optimization — the era of "spray and pray" mass advertising is over
Public Relations
Earned media — coverage the brand doesn't pay for directly; more credible because a third party is saying it
PR tools: Press releases, media pitching, community events, thought leadership, influencer relationships
Crisis communication: How a brand responds to a crisis matters as much as what caused it — speed, transparency, and accountability
Ethics: Hidden sponsorships, misleading claims, and manipulative imagery all backfire in today's transparent digital environment

Week 5 · Ch. 15

Personal Selling and Sales Promotions

The Personal Selling Process
1. Prospecting: Identify potential customers — research who has the need, budget, and authority to buy
2. Approach: Make initial contact — first impression is critical; know the customer before you call
3. Presentation: Tailor the pitch to this customer's specific needs — show you understand their problem
4. Handle objections: Address concerns without being defensive — objections mean the customer is engaged
5. Close: Ask for the business — many salespeople lose deals by failing to close explicitly
6. Follow up: Ensure satisfaction and build the relationship — the next sale is always in the follow-up
Sales Promotions
Short-term incentives designed to drive immediate action — most effective when coordinated with advertising
Consumer promotions: Coupons, rebates, samples, contests, loyalty programs, bundle deals
Trade promotions: Allowances and discounts to retailers for stocking and featuring products
Promotions drive volume — but used too frequently they train customers to never pay full price
Ethics: No hidden fees, no misleading "original" prices, no deceptive expiration dates on offers

Week 5 · Ch. 16 & 17

Digital Marketing and Distribution

Digital and Social Media Marketing
Facebook/Instagram: Largest reach; visual storytelling; highly targeted paid ads; strong for B2C
LinkedIn: B2B marketing; thought leadership; professional audience; lead generation
TikTok/YouTube: Video-first; reaches younger audiences; influencer-driven discovery
SEO and SEM: Organic and paid search — captures demand that already exists
Mobile marketing: Geotargeting, push notifications, apps, SMS — real-time, location-aware, always on
Data ethics and privacy: Transparent opt-in, clear privacy policies, and compliance with GDPR/CCPA are non-negotiable
Distribution Channels
Distribution connects producers to consumers — a great product that customers can't find or buy easily will fail
Direct channels: Manufacturer to consumer — D2C brands, Tesla, most SaaS companies
Indirect channels: Intermediaries (wholesalers, retailers, brokers) add value through logistics, market access, and expertise
Channel selection affects cost, speed, control, and the customer experience — it is a strategic decision, not an afterthought
Promotion and distribution must be aligned — advertising a product customers can't find is money wasted

Retailing — The Front Line of Consumer Access

What Retailers Do
Retailers are the last link in the distribution chain — they sell directly to end consumers for personal use
They add value by breaking bulk, offering convenience, providing service, and gathering feedback from consumers
Store-based: Department stores (Nordstrom), Discount (Target, Walmart), Specialty (REI, Apple Store), Supermarkets, Warehouse clubs (Costco)
Non-store: E-commerce, mobile commerce, social commerce, direct mail, vending machines, home shopping TV
Retailer Strategy Decisions
Target audience: Who is the core customer? Everything else follows from this decision.
Location: Physical — traffic, visibility, co-tenancy; Digital — SEO, platform presence, user experience
Merchandising: Which products to carry, how to display them, how to manage inventory
Store atmosphere: Layout, lighting, music, scent — all shape perception and purchasing behavior
Pricing and promotions: Everyday low pricing (Walmart) vs. high-low promotions (Macy's) — strategic choice with brand implications

Week 6 · Ch. 18

Wholesaling — Behind-the-Scenes Supply Chain

What Wholesalers Do
Wholesalers connect manufacturers to retailers — they buy in bulk and resell in smaller quantities
They are invisible to most consumers but essential to the efficiency of supply chains
Value-added services: Warehousing, transportation, financing and credit, marketing support, risk assumption
Types of Wholesalers
Merchant wholesalers: Take title to goods — buy, store, and resell. Full-service (carry full product lines, extend credit) or limited-service (cash and carry)
Brokers and agents: Facilitate transactions without taking title — earn a commission; common in real estate, food, and financial products
Manufacturer sales branches: Wholesaling units owned by the manufacturer — gives more control over distribution
Costco is a unique hybrid — functions as both a retailer and a wholesaler, selling in bulk directly to consumers and small businesses
Week 6 · Ch. 19

Sustainable and Purpose-Driven Marketing

Sustainable Marketing
Meeting present consumer needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs — it's strategy, not charity
ESG framework — Environmental: Carbon footprint, resource use, packaging, supply chain emissions
ESG — Social: Labor practices, diversity, community investment, product safety
ESG — Governance: Board composition, executive pay, transparency, anti-corruption practices
ESG ratings are now used by institutional investors — poor ESG performance raises cost of capital
Purpose-Driven Brands
Purpose-driven brands embed their values into every customer touchpoint — not just their ad campaigns
TOMS Shoes: "One for One" giving model built into the business model itself — not an add-on
Ben & Jerry's: Social and environmental advocacy is core to brand identity — not marketing
IKEA: Committed to being climate-positive by 2030 — solar energy, sustainable forestry, product recyclability
Gen Z and Millennials increasingly choose brands based on values alignment — sustainability is now a purchase criterion, not a differentiator
