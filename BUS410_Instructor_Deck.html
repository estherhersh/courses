What Is Strategic Management?

Week 1 · Ch. 1

Strategy vs. Strategic Management

Strategy
A long-term, higher-level plan that sets direction without specifying every detail
Guides how a firm allocates resources to create and sustain a competitive advantage
Combines analytical rigor (the science) and creative judgment (the art) — neither alone is sufficient
Strategic Management
The ongoing process of analyzing, deciding, and acting to achieve long-term success
Involves the full leadership team — not just the CEO
Asks three questions: Where are we now? Where do we want to go? How do we get there?
Discussion Questions
What is the difference between strategic management and strategy?
What business that you visit regularly seems to have the most successful business model? What makes it work?

Week 1 · Ch. 2

Vision, Mission, and Values

The Three Anchors
Vision: The organization's aspired future state — where it is ultimately trying to go
Mission: What the company does, for whom, and how — its current purpose and function
Values: The principles that guide behavior and decisions throughout the organization
A strong mission is specific, actionable, and distinguishable from competitors — not generic
What Makes a Strong Mission?
States who the customer is and what value the firm provides
Gives employees a north star for decision-making
Avoids vague language like "world-class" or "best-in-class" with no concrete meaning
Example — Nike: "Bring inspiration and innovation to every athlete in the world" — then defines "athlete" as anyone with a body
Discussion Question
What are indicators of a strong mission? Find your company's mission statement — does it meet that bar?
Week 1 · Ch. 2

Measuring Performance: Beyond Profit

The Balanced Scorecard
Profit and stock price alone are incomplete — they are lagging indicators that don't show why performance changed
Financial: ROI, revenue growth, profit margin
Customer: Satisfaction scores, retention, market share
Internal processes: Operational efficiency, quality, cycle time
Learning and growth: Employee training, innovation, capability building
Competitive Advantage
A firm has a competitive advantage when it creates more economic value than its rivals
Economic value created = Customer's willingness to pay minus the firm's cost to deliver
Unlike profit, competitive advantage is not easily distorted by short-term decisions or accounting choices
A firm can be temporarily unprofitable and still hold a competitive advantage — and vice versa
Discussion Questions
How should executives analyze the performance of their organization? What is competitive advantage and how is it calculated?

Week 1 · Ch. 3

Porter's Five Forces

The Five Forces
Rivalry among competitors: How intense is competition in the industry? High rivalry compresses margins
Threat of new entrants: How easy is it for new firms to enter? Low barriers = ongoing competitive threat
Bargaining power of buyers: Can customers force prices down or demand better terms?
Bargaining power of suppliers: Can suppliers raise prices or reduce quality?
Threat of substitutes: Can customers switch to a different product that meets the same need?
Using the Framework
The stronger each force, the harder it is to earn strong profits in that industry
Identify your industry first — the forces apply differently at the industry level vs. firm level
Strategic groups map direct competitors by strategy — not all firms in an industry face the same pressure
Discussion Questions
How is Porter's Five Forces used to perform a competitive analysis of an industry?
What are strategic groups and how are they useful to evaluate the competitive environment?

Evaluating the Internal

Week 1 · Ch. 4

Resources, Capabilities, and VRIO

Tangible vs. Intangible Resources
Tangible: Physical assets — property, equipment, cash, inventory. Visible and easy to copy
Intangible: Brand, culture, patents, know-how, relationships. Hard to see, harder to replicate
Long-term competitive advantage is more often built on intangibles — they are harder for rivals to acquire
The VRIO Framework
Valuable: Does this resource help the firm exploit an opportunity or neutralize a threat?
Rare: Do few or no competitors possess it?
Inimitable: Is it costly or difficult for competitors to copy or substitute?
Organized: Is the firm set up to actually capture the value from this resource?
Only resources that pass all four tests generate a sustained competitive advantage
Discussion Question
What tools can be used to evaluate the internal environment? How is the VRIO framework applied to strategic resources?
Week 1 · Ch. 4

Value Chain Analysis

Primary Activities
Inbound logistics: Receiving, storing, and distributing inputs
Operations: Transforming inputs into the final product
Outbound logistics: Delivering the product to customers
Marketing and sales: Getting customers to buy
Service: Post-sale support that maintains value
Support Activities
Firm infrastructure: Finance, legal, executive management
Human resource management: Hiring, training, compensation
Technology development: R&D, process improvement, IT
Procurement: Purchasing inputs at the best cost and quality
Value chain analysis identifies where your company creates value and where competitors may have an edge

Week 1 · Ch. 5

SWOT: Pulling the Analysis Together

Internal (Firm Controls)
Strengths: Resources and capabilities that give the firm an advantage — what it does better than rivals
Weaknesses: Internal gaps or vulnerabilities that put the firm at a disadvantage
Internal factors come from your VRIO and value chain work — not from opinions
External (Firm Monitors)
Opportunities: External trends or conditions the firm could exploit — from PESTEL and Five Forces
Threats: External forces that could harm the firm's competitive position
SWOT is a decision-making tool, not a checklist — it generates strategic issues that demand a response
Discussion Question
How is the SWOT framework developed and used for determining strategic issues and strategies?

Week 2 · Ch. 5

Identifying Strategic Issues

A strategic issue is a critical challenge or opportunity revealed by your SWOT, PESTEL, or competitor analysis that requires a deliberate response from leadership
Strategic issues are not surface problems — they are systemic conditions that affect long-term success if not addressed
Examples: a key competitor entering your market segment, a regulatory shift that changes your cost structure, a technology that makes your product obsolete
The right question: Is this issue significant enough that ignoring it could change the firm's competitive position in 3–5 years?
Discussion Question
How can we identify a strategic issue? What distinguishes a strategic issue from an operational problem?

Week 2 · Ch. 6

Cost Leadership vs. Differentiation: Tradeoffs

Cost Leadership
Advantage: Creates barriers to entry — rivals can't match prices without losing money
Advantage: Protects against buyer power — customers have fewer low-cost alternatives
Risk: Technology or process innovation by a rival can eliminate the cost gap overnight
Risk: A pure price race erodes margins for the whole industry, not just the laggards
Differentiation
Advantage: Creates customer loyalty — buyers who value the differentiation are less price-sensitive
Advantage: Commands premium pricing — higher margins offset lower volume
Risk: Customers may decide the premium isn't worth it — especially in economic downturns
Risk: Competitors imitate the differentiating feature and close the gap

Week 3 · Ch. 7

First Movers, Fast Followers, and Cooperative Strategies

First Mover vs. Fast Follower
First mover: Enters the market before rivals — builds brand recognition, secures distribution, and sets the standard
First mover risks: High R&D cost, uncertain demand, educating the market at your expense
Fast follower: Learns from the first mover's mistakes, enters with an improved product at lower cost
Example: Google was not the first search engine — it was a fast follower that outexecuted early movers
Cooperative Strategies
Strategic alliances: Two firms share resources or capabilities without merging — reduces risk and cost
Joint ventures: Two firms create a shared entity to pursue a specific opportunity
Co-opetition: Competing firms collaborate on a shared challenge — e.g., auto manufacturers collaborating on charging infrastructure
Apply to Your Company
What type of innovation is your company currently pursuing? Is there an opportunity for a cooperative strategy to accelerate it?

Week 3 · Ch. 9

Why Go International — and the CAGE Framework

Reasons to Go International
Access to new customers in markets where domestic growth has slowed
Lower production costs through manufacturing in lower-wage countries
Risk diversification — not all markets move together
Access to specialized resources, talent, or technology concentrated in certain regions
The CAGE Framework
Cultural: Language, religion, social norms — how different is the target country culturally?
Administrative: Legal systems, trade agreements, colonial ties, regulatory environment
Geographic: Physical distance, time zones, landlocked vs. coastal, climate
Economic: Income levels, consumer purchasing power, cost of labor and capital
Greater CAGE distance = higher risk of failure. Start with markets closest to home before entering distant ones

Week 3 · Financial Analysis

Financial Analysis and

Week 3 · Financial Analysis

Building a Strategic Financial Analysis

What You Need to Build
Annual budget: Revenue projections, operating expenses, and capital expenditures — presented in a table
3–5 year forecast: Project revenues, expenses, and cash flows — state your assumptions explicitly
Feasibility analysis: Assess whether a key strategic initiative is financially viable
Key Metrics
ROI (Return on Investment): Net profit divided by cost of investment — shows efficiency of capital use
NPV (Net Present Value): Present value of future cash flows minus the initial investment — positive NPV means the investment creates value
IRR (Internal Rate of Return): The discount rate that makes NPV equal zero — higher IRR = more attractive investment
Payback period: How long until the investment pays for itself
Discussion Question
What type of information do you need to collect to perform a financial analysis? Have your Week 1–2 strategies revealed any need to revise your approach?

Week 4 · Ch. 10

Organizational Structure and Strategy Execution

Why Structure Matters
Strategy only works when the organization is structured to execute it — structure determines who decides what, and how fast
Division of labor: How tasks are divided to maximize efficiency
Reporting relationships: Who answers to whom
Decision-making authority: Centralized (top-down) vs. decentralized (distributed)
Common Structures
Functional: Organized by job function (Marketing, Finance, Operations) — efficient but siloed
Divisional: Organized by product line, geography, or customer — flexible but can duplicate resources
Matrix: Dual reporting — employees report to both a functional manager and a project manager
Boundaryless / Flat: Minimal hierarchy — faster decisions, common in startups and technology firms
Discussion Questions

Week 4 · Ch. 11

Corporate Governance and Ethics

Corporate Governance
The system by which companies are directed and controlled — balances the interests of shareholders, executives, employees, and the public
Board of directors: Elected by shareholders to oversee management and protect stakeholder interests
The agency problem: Executives may act in their own interest rather than shareholders' — governance structures exist to align incentives
Codes of conduct, ethics hotlines, and executive compensation tied to long-term performance are common governance tools
Corporate Social Responsibility
CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility): A firm's obligation to act in the interests of society, not just shareholders
Shared value: Michael Porter's argument that business can generate profit and social benefit simultaneously — not a tradeoff
ESG reporting has become standard for public companies — investors use it to assess long-term risk and responsibility
Ethical failures (Enron, Wells Fargo, Theranos) destroy value faster than almost any other business risk
Discussion Questions
What is corporate social responsibility and its strategic role for a firm?
What is the role of the firm's board of directors as it relates to ethical behavior?

Week 5 · KPIs

What Makes a Good KPI

Critical Success Factors First
A Critical Success Factor (CSF) is an activity the organization must perform well to achieve its mission — it comes before the KPI
Every KPI should map directly to a CSF, which maps to a strategic objective — if the chain breaks, the metric is noise
Example chain: Objective "Grow market share" → CSF "Customer retention" → KPI "90% customer satisfaction rating within 12 months"
SMART KPIs
Specific: Precisely defined — no ambiguity about what is being measured
Measurable: Expressed in numbers, percentages, or clear outcomes
Achievable: Realistic given resources and timeframe
Relevant: Directly tied to a strategic objective or CSF
Time-bound: Has a clear measurement deadline
Discussion Questions
What is the relationship between strategic objectives and KPIs? What makes a good KPI? What elements should we be tracking to measure firm performance?

Week 6 · Strategic Communication

Communicating Strategy Effectively

Why Communication Fails
Most strategic plans fail not because the strategy is wrong — but because no one in the organization understands or believes in it
Generic language ("world-class," "customer-centric") communicates nothing — specificity builds credibility
Strategy communicated once is forgotten. It must be embedded in decisions, meetings, and resource allocation consistently
What Strong Strategic Communication Does
Tailors the message to the audience — investors, employees, and customers need different things from the same strategy
Uses narrative — people remember stories, not bullet points
Makes trade-offs explicit — a good strategy says no to things. That clarity is itself a signal of leadership
Connects every initiative back to the strategic logic — "we are doing X because it addresses Y, which advances Z"
